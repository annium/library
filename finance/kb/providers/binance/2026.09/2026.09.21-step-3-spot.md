## The drift, and what each was worth

**A documented status that threw rather than mis-mapped.** Spot documents `PENDING_NEW` — an order in
an order list waiting for its working order to fill — and nothing mapped it. The interesting half is
what that cost: the lookup **throws** on an unknown value, so this was never a status arriving wrong.
It was one pending leg failing the parse of the entire list it arrived in, taking the other orders
with it. Mapped to `New`, which is what the contract describes, and pinned.

**The same guarded quotient in three converters, with only one arm ever run.** An executed price is
`executedSum / executedQty` guarded by a zero check, in get-order, init-order and order-update. Every
fixture on hand had filled something, so only the dividing arm had ever executed — and nothing filled
is not an edge case, it is what a new order looks like. Collapsing any of the three guards would have
been a divide-by-zero on the commonest state an order has, and no test would have said so.

**Five drop rules that nothing named.** A trade missing its order id, its symbol, or the asset its
commission was taken in; an order update with no order id; an exchange info answer carrying one of its
two collections and not the other. Each was written and each was unexercised. Two of them had a test
that looked like coverage and was not: one fixture satisfying every arm of an `OR` at once proves no
arm individually.

## What the mutation checks actually showed

Nine facts. **Six died to a mutation.** Three could not be mutated at all, and that is worth naming
rather than counting as untested: removing the blank-check on a trade's symbol or commission asset, or
either half of the exchange-info condition, **fails the build** — the model takes non-nullable strings
and the check is what proves non-nullness to the compiler.

A check the compiler needs cannot be quietly deleted. That is a stronger guarantee than a test, and a
weaker one than a test plus the compiler, which is what those three now have.

## Two things the run did to itself, both already in the skill

Both were met again here, which is the argument for them being written down rather than remembered.

**A mutant that does not compile is skipped, not tested.** Seven mutations were applied in one batch;
the build failed and the runner, given `--no-build`, reported the previous binary as green. Splitting
them one per build is what produced the six kills above.

**Restore by reversing the edit.** The reversal here was scripted, and the script replaced an empty
string — which inserts at position zero rather than undoing anything, and left two source files with
junk prepended. Caught by reading the tree, not by a test. The recovery was only cheap because the sole
uncommitted source change was one dictionary entry that could be re-applied exactly.

And a third, the same shape as the first: the full suite was run with `--no-build` while the binaries
were left over from a mutant build, and reported a failure that did not exist in the source. Rebuild
before believing a run that follows a mutation loop.

## One verifier finding that did not survive

The verifier reported a trade with `orderId: 0` as a case the drop rule should catch. It does not, and
should not: the rule is about a value that never arrived, and a zero is a value. The test was rewritten
to **omit** each field rather than blank it. Written down because the difference between "absent" and
"empty" is exactly the kind of thing a plausible-sounding review finding gets wrong.
